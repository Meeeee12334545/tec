[Heading1] How to use this guide
This guide converts two documents — the Educational Advisory Services business plan and the School Capability & Workload Diagnostic Matrix V1 — into an implementable sequence. It assumes nothing is set up yet.
It is organised so you can work through it in order:
[ListParagraph] • Sections 1–2 establish where you are starting from and what you are aiming at. Read these together, once.
[ListParagraph] • Section 3 is the requirements register — everything that must exist before you can trade safely. Work through it as a checklist.
[ListParagraph] • Section 4 turns the Diagnostic Matrix from a document into a deliverable product. This is the commercial core.
[ListParagraph] • Section 5 is the phased implementation plan with gates. Do not skip gates.
[ListParagraph] • Sections 6–7 cover pricing and risk.
[ListParagraph] • Section 8 is the decision register — nine decisions that block everything else. Start here if you only read one page.
A note on scope
This guide sets out structure, sequence and the questions you need answered. It is not legal, tax, accounting or insurance advice, and it deliberately does not state registration thresholds, fees or premium figures — those change, and getting them from a professional who knows your circumstances is part of the plan rather than a detour from it.
Section 3 and Appendix C are written so you can take them straight into a meeting with an accountant, a lawyer and an insurance broker.

Beth is eight months into a senior, full-time role at Hillbrook. This single fact shapes the first twelve months more than anything else in either document.

[ListParagraph] • Approaching schools inside Hillbrook's competitive set carries real professional risk

[Heading2] 3.4 Employment and conflict of interest — Beth
This is the highest-risk area in the whole plan and it must be handled before anything external happens. Handled early it is a manageable administrative matter. Handled late, or discovered, it can end a career and the business at the same time.
Requirement
What it involves
Read the Hillbrook contract properly
Look specifically for: secondary employment or outside work clauses, restraint of trade, intellectual property assignment, conflict of interest and disclosure obligations. If anything is ambiguous, one hour of employment law advice is cheap insurance.
Decide the disclosure position
In most independent schools an early, written, low-key disclosure to the Principal is far safer than being discovered later — and often results in permission with conditions attached. The alternative is carrying a risk you cannot control.
Establish a hard IP boundary
Nothing created in Hillbrook time, on Hillbrook systems, or for Hillbrook purposes enters the business. Build every template from scratch, on personal equipment, outside work hours. Keep a dated record showing you did.
Fix the current-employer marketing problem
Past employers can be listed factually as career history. A current employer presented as a credential implies endorsement. Remove Hillbrook from any client-facing credential list unless written permission is given.
Agree an off-limits school list

Set delivery windows honestly

[Heading1] 4. The diagnostic: from document to product
[Heading2] 4.1 What V1 is, and what it is not
Matrix V1 is strong thinking. Its central insight — asking how a process actually happens rather than whether a document nominally exists — is exactly right, and it is what separates this from a generic school review. The worked example on Year 5 English is the clearest articulation of the value proposition in either document.
But it is currently a document describing a diagnostic, not a diagnostic. To be delivered repeatably, by two people, at a consistent standard, it needs four layers:

[Heading2] 4.2 Layer 1 — the instrument
Ten domains is too many for a single engagement. Split into a core set that runs every time and modules that run when indicated by the first conversation.
Core — run every engagement
Modules — run when indicated
1. School direction and expectations
2. Curriculum planning process
3. Unit development and delivery readiness
4. Assessment development
5. Marking, moderation and reporting
8. Workload and working hours
6. Meetings and collaboration
7. Systems, platforms and resource management
9. Staff capability and confidence
10. AI and digital workload reduction
Three additions each domain needs:
[ListParagraph] • Follow-up prompts. “How long does it take?” produces a guess. “Walk me through the last time you did it, start to finish” produces the truth. Every question needs its second-level probe written down, or the diagnostic depends entirely on who is running it.
[ListParagraph] • Quantification anchors. Offer ranges rather than open numbers, so answers are comparable between respondents and between schools. This is what makes benchmarking possible later.
[ListParagraph] • An evidence request. Per domain: show me the template, show me where it is stored, show me the last one you completed. What people describe and what exists routinely differ — and the gap is itself a finding.

[Heading3] Psychological safety
Matrix V1 correctly flags that the workload section must not feel like scrutiny. Formalise this rather than leaving it to instinct. Open every session with the same script:
[ListParagraph] • What this is — a review of processes and systems, not of people
[ListParagraph] • What it is not — not a performance process, not commissioned to find underperformers
[ListParagraph] • Who sees what — no individual is identified in any report; survey data is aggregated
[ListParagraph] • Why they should be candid — the findings determine what the school invests in fixing
[ListParagraph] • What happens next — and when they will see the outcome
Put the same content in writing as a participant information and consent statement. It protects the staff, the school and you.
[Heading2] 4.4 Layer 3 — analysis
Findings must be comparable, defensible and repeatable. Three mechanisms do this.
[Heading3] A two-axis maturity rating
Rate each domain on two independent axes rather than a single score. A single score out of ten is indefensible under challenge and tells a school nothing about what to do.
Rating
Process maturity
Workload impact
1
Absent — no defined process
Negligible — minimal staff time
2
Ad hoc — depends entirely on individuals
Low
3

Moderate — noticeable but contained
4

5

Why two axes is the commercial engine
A domain that is high maturity but high workload is a different sale from one that is low maturity and low workload. The first needs process simplification; the second needs capability building. Without the second axis you cannot tell them apart, and you will recommend the wrong thing.
It also protects the philosophy in the business plan. A school can score well on maturity and still be exhausting its staff — which lets you deliver difficult findings without implying anyone is failing.
[Heading3] Priority matrix
Plot workload impact against ease of improvement to produce four action categories:
Easy to improve
Hard to improve
High workload impact
Immediate wins — do first, builds trust and proves value fast
Major projects — scope as staged engagements
Low workload impact
Housekeeping — school can self-manage
Deprioritise — name it and move on
[Heading3] Time quantification
Convert reported time into annual staff-hours per process. This is the number that makes the value case concrete: “this school spends roughly 400 staff hours a year on duplicated unit planning” is a proposition a Business Manager can act on. “Planning is inefficient” is not.
[Heading3] Triangulation rule
Never report a finding sourced from a single respondent. Every finding in the report must be corroborated by at least two of: interview, focus group, survey data, document review. Write this rule into the method section of the report — it is what makes the findings survive a defensive leadership team.
[Heading2] 4.5 Layer 4 — the findings report
The report is both the product of the diagnostic and the sales instrument for everything that follows. Fixed structure:
[ListParagraph] • Method — what was done, who was consulted, and the limits of the review
[ListParagraph] • What is working — genuine strengths, named specifically. The plan's philosophy demands this and it buys the licence to say the rest
[ListParagraph] • The maturity picture — two-axis rating across domains, presented visually
[ListParagraph] • Priority findings — five to eight maximum, each using the action record fields from Matrix V1
[ListParagraph] • Quantified workload exposure — annual staff-hours by process
[ListParagraph] • Recommended sequence — immediate wins first, then structural projects
[ListParagraph] • What we can provide, and what the school should do itself
Section 7 is the most commercially important part of the report
Deliberately recommending things you do not sell — and telling a school plainly which fixes it should handle internally — is what makes the things you do sell credible. It is also a direct expression of the rule already in the business plan: only ever offer a service that proactively benefits the client.
Consultants who recommend only their own services get one engagement. Consultants who tell a school what not to buy get referred.
Cap the findings at eight. A report with twenty findings gets filed; a report with six gets acted on.

The one decision to get right: is the diagnostic free or paid?
The case for free is that it removes friction and generates pipeline. The case for paid is stronger: a school that has paid for a diagnostic will act on it, and a free one gets thanked for and filed. Paying also forces the school to nominate an internal owner, which is the single best predictor of whether anything changes.
Recommendation: keep the first meeting free exactly as the plan states, price the diagnostic, and credit the diagnostic fee against the first deliverable package. That removes the objection without devaluing the work, and it makes the conversion to paid delivery the default rather than a second sale.

[Heading1] 5. Sequenced implementation
Five phases with gates. Each gate is an exit criterion — do not begin the next phase until it is met. The gates exist because the most common failure mode for a new consultancy is approaching the market before it can safely deliver, and then either declining work or delivering badly.
The school calendar dominates everything
Budget decisions for the following year are typically made in Term 4. That makes September to November the buying window — approaches landing then reach schools when money is actually being allocated.
Late Term 1 and Term 3 are realistic delivery windows; Term 4 and the start of Term 1 are not. Given Beth's availability is already constrained, plan approaches and delivery against this calendar from the outset rather than discovering it in month eight.

[Heading1] 6. Commercial model
This section gives you the method rather than the numbers. Any figures quoted here would be invented, and pricing set from invented figures is the fastest way to underprice a practice permanently.
[Heading2] 6.1 Establish the floor
Take Beth's current total employment package, including superannuation and leave. Divide it by the number of days she could realistically deliver in a year. Then multiply by roughly two and a half to three.
The multiplier covers what employment hides: non-billable time (scoping, proposals, admin, marketing — usually more than half of all working time), overheads, insurance, tax, no paid leave, no sick leave, no superannuation contribution, and the risk premium of irregular income.
Consultants who price from their salary alone go broke slowly and never understand why. That number is your floor, not your price.
[Heading2] 6.2 Establish the ceiling
The cost-comparison framing already in the business plan is the right instrument, and it is stronger than most consultancies have. Assemble real figures for:
[ListParagraph] • The cost of recruiting and replacing a teacher
[ListParagraph] • The cost of onboarding a new teacher into a school's systems and culture
[ListParagraph] • The value of teaching hours returned to staff, costed at an hourly rate
[ListParagraph] • The cost of the school building the same capability internally — in staff release time
If a diagnostic identifies several hundred staff hours a year lost to duplicated planning, the value case writes itself and the conversation moves off day rates entirely. This is exactly why the quantification work in Section 4.4 matters commercially.

[Heading2] 6.4 Pricing principles
[ListParagraph] • Fixed price, not hourly, wherever scope can be defined. Schools have budgets and approval processes, not hourly appetites. A fixed price is approvable; an open-ended hourly rate is not.
[ListParagraph] • Never quote before scoping. The free first meeting exists precisely so you can price properly.
[ListParagraph] • Price the package, improve margin by getting faster. This is the whole logic of productisation, and it aligns your interests with the client's.
[ListParagraph] • Milestone invoicing. Schools pay slowly. Invoice on engagement, on report delivery, and on package completion — not once at the end.
[ListParagraph] • Hold the price. Early discounting to win a first client sets a reference point you will spend two years escaping. Give a pilot away entirely for a case study if you must, but do not discount the standing rate.

[Heading2] 7.2 Family governance
You are siblings. That is a real advantage — trust, shared history, low transaction cost, and a willingness to work through difficulty that unrelated founders rarely have.
It is also the most common way small partnerships fail badly, because the things that would be explicitly negotiated between strangers get quietly assumed between family. The assumptions only surface when there is money, stress or disagreement — by which point the conversation is much harder and the relationship is what is at stake, not just the business.
Write down, before anything else happens:
[ListParagraph] • The equity split, and the reasoning behind it
[ListParagraph] • What each of you commits — in hours per week and in money
[ListParagraph] • Who decides what alone, and what requires both of you
[ListParagraph] • How each of you is paid, and when — including whether early revenue is reinvested
[ListParagraph] • Who owns the intellectual property, and what happens to it if one of you leaves
[ListParagraph] • What happens if one of you wants out, or wants to stop
[ListParagraph] • What happens if it does not work
[ListParagraph] • How you will handle a disagreement neither of you can win
Do this while you like each other
It takes an afternoon and a lawyer's review. Every founder who skipped it and later needed it says the same thing.
There is also a specific asymmetry here worth naming openly: Beth is the product and Sebastian is the operator. If equity is split on effort in year one it will look wrong by year three, and if it is split on credibility it may feel wrong in year one. Decide deliberately, write down the reasoning, and agree a review point.

[Heading2] For the accountant
[ListParagraph] • Given two founders, one employed full-time, and an intention to license IP later — which structure and why?
[ListParagraph] • Should we trade through EDS or establish separately? What are the risk and tax consequences of each?
[ListParagraph] • What is the current GST registration threshold, and is there a case for registering voluntarily before we reach it?
[ListParagraph] • How should each of us be paid, given Beth remains employed elsewhere?
[ListParagraph] • What records do we need from day one that are painful to reconstruct later?
[ListParagraph] • What are the tax consequences if the entity later licenses the diagnostic rather than delivering services?
[Heading2] For the lawyer
[ListParagraph] • Review Beth's employment contract for restraint, secondary employment, IP assignment and disclosure obligations
[ListParagraph] • Draft or review the founders' / shareholders agreement
[ListParagraph] • Draft the engagement letter and standard terms, including limitation of liability and IP licensing to clients
[ListParagraph] • Advise on privacy obligations when collecting staff workload and wellbeing data on a school's behalf
[ListParagraph] • Who owns the findings report — us or the school — and what may we reuse or publish?
[Heading2] For the insurance broker
[ListParagraph] • Professional indemnity cover for advisory work that schools will restructure processes around — what limit is appropriate?
[ListParagraph] • Public liability at the level schools typically require as a condition of site access
[ListParagraph] • Cyber and privacy cover given we will hold staff survey data
[ListParagraph] • How does run-off cover work, and when should we put it in place?
[ListParagraph] • Does cover extend to both founders, including when one is attending a school in a non-advisory role?
[Heading2] For Blue Card Services
[ListParagraph] • Does an education consultant delivering staff-facing advisory work on a school site require a blue card?
[ListParagraph] • Does Beth's teacher registration exempt her, and does that exemption extend to work outside her employment?
[ListParagraph] • Does the answer change if children are present on site during the work?

A closing observation on Domain 10
AI is currently the fastest route into a school. It is the issue leadership teams feel least equipped for, it carries visible academic integrity risk, and it has a board-level profile that curriculum mapping does not.
It is also the domain where Beth's combination of Technologies specialism, curriculum authority background and Apple Distinguished Educator standing is hardest for anyone else to match — and the one where Sebastian's involvement is most credible. Consider leading with it commercially even though the deeper, more durable value sits in Domains 2 to 5.
